## Digest

*≈ 530 words — more than half of the step's 700-word cap, which F06b (ReBeL, Student of Games, synthesis) shares; the two mechanism paragraphs (DeepStack, Libratus) are the place to cut when drafting.*

Between 2017 and 2019 three systems reached or exceeded professional strength in no-limit Texas hold'em. Before them the dominant pipeline was to abstract the game, precompute an approximate equilibrium ("blueprint") offline and map the opponent's real actions into the abstraction. Such agents lost to professionals (Claudico, −91 mbb/g in 2015 [9]) and were shown by local best response to be exploitable by more than 3,000 mbb/g, four times more than folding every hand [1, 8].

DeepStack [1] dropped the precomputed strategy. At every decision it re-solves a depth-limited lookahead from the current public state, using only its own range and the opponent's counterfactual values; a deep network estimates counterfactual values at the depth limit. With value error ε and T CFR iterations, the exploitability of the re-solved strategy is below k₁ε + k₂/√T [1, Thm 1]. The sparse action set used in play voids this bound, so low exploitability is shown empirically: the best-response probe fails to beat it. DeepStack beat 33 professionals, who were not HUNL specialists [2, 6], by 492 mbb/g over 44,852 hands, evaluated with AIVAT [10].

Libratus [2] kept the blueprint (10¹⁶¹ decision points abstracted to about 10¹², solved with MCCFR [4]) and replaced play from the third betting round on with nested safe subgame solving. Each subgame is re-solved in real time in an augmented game, and every off-tree bet is solved exactly rather than translated. The refined strategy is at most 2Δ more exploitable than the best strategy that agrees with the blueprint outside the subgames, where Δ is the error of the blueprint's value estimates [2, Thm 1; 3]. In a small test game this cut exploitability from 1,465 to 119 mbb/hand relative to action translation. The raw blueprint did not beat the previous champion bot (−8 ± 15 mbb/hand); with subgame solving the system won by 63 ± 28. Against four HUNL specialists Libratus won 147 mbb/hand over 120,000 hands [2]. This took about 25 million core-hours [4].

Pluribus [5] carried the blueprint-plus-search recipe to six-player poker, where a Nash equilibrium is neither efficiently computable nor a guarantee against losing. Its authors dropped solution concepts as the goal in favour of an AI "that empirically consistently defeats human opponents". Search is depth-limited: at each leaf every player chooses among four continuation strategies [5, 6]. The search is "unsafe", which "lacks theoretical guarantees on performance even in two-player zero-sum games" [5 SM]. Trained for 12,400 core-hours (about $144) and playing on two CPUs, it won 48 mbb/game (SE 25) against five professionals and 32 (SE 15) in the one-human format.

Two properties of this line matter for the dissertation. First, its guarantees (k₁ε + k₂/√T, 2Δ) are two-player zero-sum results, and the one multiplayer system has none. Second, none of the landmark superhuman systems adapts to its opponents. Pluribus "plays a fixed strategy that does not adapt to the observed tendencies of the opponents". Its authors give two reasons: an exploitative deviation "opens oneself up to exploitation", and existing techniques "require too many samples to be competitive with human ability outside of small games" [5]. Libratus, "to a first approximation, … did not do opponent exploitation" [2]. Both papers name conservative (safe) exploitation [11] as the only exception.

## Gaps
- **G-a (C1): the landmark systems do not adapt, by design and because adaptation was too data-hungry.** [2] and [5] state it and give the reasons (counter-exploitation risk; "too many samples … outside of small games"). DeepStack's paper does not discuss opponents at all [1]. Consistent with `lit_gaps.md`: phrase it as "none of the landmark superhuman systems adapts to its opponents", **not** "nobody adapts". Online opponent modelling that exploits while staying near equilibrium exists, but in small two-player games (GSCU, StratFormer, AlphaExploitem — see `lit_gaps.md` C1). The sample-efficiency reason in [5] is the opening for C1's real-time inference.
- **G-b (C2): the exploitability guarantees of the search line are two-player zero-sum results.** k₁ε + k₂/√T [1] and 2Δ [2, 3] rest on the minimax structure. Pluribus has no bound, uses unsafe search, and its authors claim only empirical strength "despite the lack of known strong theoretical guarantees on performance in multiplayer games" [5]. Per `lit_gaps.md` C2, N-player safety *notions* exist (equal share, team-maxmin, baseline-relative regret), but no N-player exploiting agent uses one. Chapter I should say "the landmark N-player system offers no guarantee", not "no N-player safety notion exists".
- **G-c (C3): the landmark systems were evaluated by head-to-head win rate against a handful of humans, plus a best-response lower bound that exists only in the two-player case.** DeepStack and Libratus use LBR/exploitability probes and AIVAT [1, 2]. Pluribus has no exploitability measure in six-player play; individual human rates "could not be determined with statistical significance" [5]. None measures adaptation (speed, gain against weak opponents) or robustness to coordinated opponents. Consistent with `lit_gaps.md` C3 (narrowed; coalition-aware robustness metrics not found).
- **G-d (C1 → C2): the belief machinery tracks cards, not opponents.** DeepStack's (range, opponent counterfactual values) state [1] and the depth-limited leaf construction [5, 6] model the hidden *cards* under an equilibrium assumption. Opponent models inside depth-limited search exist only with a *given* model, two-player (ABD, `lit_gaps.md`). The chapter's claim that ReBeL's public belief state can be widened to opponent *types* is the thesis's proposal, not a published result.
