## Digest

In a two-player zero-sum game a Nash equilibrium strategy guarantees the game value against any opponent, but it is blind by design. It plays the same way against an expert and against an opponent who folds to every bet, so it leaves value unclaimed against sub-optimal play. Opponent modeling addresses this: it infers a specific opponent's strategy from observed play and deviates from equilibrium to exploit it. The deviation brings back risk, because a strategy tailored to a wrong model can itself be exploited. This exploitation–safety tension runs through the field [7, 8].

The dominant formulation is Bayesian. A prior over opponent strategies is updated with each observed action. The prior is either a finite menu of opponent types or a Dirichlet distribution over the action probabilities at each decision point; Dirichlet–multinomial conjugacy reduces the update to adding counts. For poker, Southey et al. [1] made the imperfect-information complication explicit. Actions are observed, but private cards are revealed only at a showdown, so each observation has to be marginalized over the hands the opponent might have held. Ganzfried and Sun [2] showed that the expected payoff against a posterior distribution over opponent strategies equals the payoff against its mean. Bayesian exploitation therefore reduces to a best response against one averaged opponent, which gives an exact algorithm for a class of imperfect-information games. Other lines of work build the opponent model by adjusting a precomputed equilibrium towards observed action frequencies [5], or model implicitly: they estimate online the value of a fixed portfolio of counter-strategies rather than the opponent's strategy itself [6]. Across these families the trade-offs are convergence speed, robustness to opponents outside the model class, interpretability and computation.

Two limitations are now documented. First, a model that fits the data has not necessarily identified the opponent. Ganzfried [3] shows that standard Bayesian responses are not consistent: best response to a posterior built from sampled candidates, maximum-a-posteriori response and Thompson-style response all can fail to converge to the opponent's true strategy, even against a static opponent and with unlimited data. In a rock–paper–scissors example the posterior collapses onto a single sampled strategy although the true strategy is their exact average. The remedy estimates the posterior mode in sequence form [11, 12], where valid strategies form a polytope defined by linear constraints. With a Dirichlet prior the estimation problem is convex, solvable by projected gradient descent, and provably convergent under identifiability, positive prior density and infinitely repeated visits to every information set. The price is optimality: the mode is consistent but not payoff-optimal, whereas the payoff-optimal mean is intractable. So far the guarantee is demonstrated only on Kuhn poker, against static opponents drawn from the prior; opponents outside the prior and dynamic opponents are explicitly left open.

Second, real opponents are non-stationary. A survey of multi-agent learning classifies ways of coping with changing opponents as ignoring, forgetting, responding to target models, learning models, and theory of mind [9]. Change-point detection [10] implements forgetting, but what an agent should do once a change is flagged has received far less analysis than the detection itself: how much history to discard and how far to retreat towards equilibrium.

Safety results bound how much exploitation may risk [4, 13] (§ 1.5), but they take the opponent model as given. No existing framework couples the three stages — detect the opponent's type or strategy, adapt with a deviation scaled to the confidence of that inference, and evaluate the outcome against exact references — in a single loop. Each stage is studied in isolation, usually on one small game.

## Gaps
- **G-a (C1): opponent modeling is not closed into a detect → adapt → evaluate loop.** Inference [1, 2, 3], safe deviation [4, 13] and non-stationarity handling [9, 10] are developed and evaluated separately. None of them scales the size of the deviation to the confidence of the current inference within one loop. The two surveys [7, 8] present them as separate method families.
- **G-b (C1): consistency results cover only static, in-prior opponents on a toy game.** [3] proves convergence for opponents drawn from a known prior, tests on Kuhn only, and lists opponents outside the prior and dynamic opponents as future work. Real-time cost is not analysed: the paper expects scalability, while this step measured refit times that grow with the length of the history.
- **G-c (C1): misspecified type menus fail silently.** A product-of-likelihoods posterior over a finite menu [1, 2] reports high confidence in the best *relative* fit, with no "none of the above". None of the sources above measures how long such a confident wrong belief persists; step 07's own sweep does (see Own evidence).
- **G-d (C1 → C2): reaction to detected change is under-studied.** [9] classifies forgetting as a strategy and [10] provides detection, but the cost of the reaction (a full reset and retreat to equilibrium versus partial forgetting) is not analysed together with the detector's false-alarm rate.
- **G-e (C3): evaluation of exploiters is rarely bracketed by exact references.** Results are typically reported as win rates against a pool. Placing every realized value between the exact equilibrium value and the exact best-response ceiling (possible in Kuhn/Leduc) separates model error from exploitation error. This was done in step 07; I could not confirm it as a gap in the literature from the sources read, so it needs a literature check before being claimed.

## Own evidence
- **Headroom:** on Kuhn, equilibrium play leaves 0.21–0.23 chips/hand unexploited in the first seat (0.11–0.28 across both seats) against three exploitable stereotypes, and ≈ 0.004 against an equilibrium opponent. Source: report_en §3, `implementation/step07/exploration/exploitation_opportunity.py`. Caveat: exact computation, but against a 20 000-iteration CFR approximation of equilibrium; toy game.
- **Model class matters; underfit models self-harm:** on Leduc, the type-based model stays within 3 % of the exact best-response ceiling on all 9 opponent types, while the per-situation (continuous) model *loses* to an equilibrium opponent (−0.175 vs a −0.083 ceiling, ≈ 5.5 SE). Source: `implementation/step07/implementation/results/leduc_scale.json`, 5 seeds × 20 000 hands. Caveat: the type menu contains the true type (in-menu), which is the easy case for the type model.
- **Confident and wrong:** with a 50/50 rock–maniac blend outside a 4-type menu, a wrong type still leads after hand 100 in 40/300 runs (13 %) and after hand 200 in 14/300. Lock-in hand: median 23, 90th percentile 125, worst 461. Source: report_en §4.3, `exploration/robustness_sweep.py`. Caveat: Kuhn only, one blend.
- **Reaction to change cuts both ways:** after a mid-match style switch, change-point forgetting turns a loss into a profit on Kuhn (−0.106 → +0.211 per hand) but cuts profit on Leduc (+1.834 → +0.552), because of about 55–63 false resets per 20 000-hand match. Source: `results/{kuhn,leduc}_scale.json`, report_en §10. Caveat: 5 seeds, one switch type, BOCPD on a single aggression signal.
- **Consistency in practice:** the sequence-form MAP estimator recovers Kuhn strategies to total-variation distance 0.004–0.021, but was not run online because refit time grows with history. Source: report_en §8. Caveat: Kuhn only, no timing table in the summary.

## To verify
- [3] is an arXiv preprint (v7 at the time of checking), with no peer-reviewed venue found. Confirm the status before presenting it as an established result, and cite the version number.
- In [2] the "mean" is taken over the opponent's mixed strategies (probabilities of pure strategies). Chapter I should not say "average the action probabilities at each information set" without that qualification.
- [3] Proposition 4 also requires the true strategy to lie in the *interior* of the feasible set. Include it if the conditions are listed.
- [7, 8]: only metadata was verified. Read the relevant sections before attributing the exploitation–safety framing or a taxonomy to them.
- [13]: existence verified, text not read. Check that it defines ε-safe strategies as stated before using it to date "safe exploitation".
- Leduc's size relative to Kuhn: the chapter says "roughly two orders of magnitude more situations". Check the infoset counts (commonly 12 vs 936) before quoting a number.
- G-e (exact-reference bracketing as a gap) needs a literature check; see Gaps.
- The EN one-pager claim that "the convex guarantee breaks for N > 2" is unsourced; do not use it in Chapter I without a source.
