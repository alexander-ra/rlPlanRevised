## Digest

The superhuman poker systems of 2017–2019 answered the central difficulty of imperfect-information games — a state has no well-defined value, because the value of an action depends on the probability with which it is played [3] — in two ways: learned values at the depth limit with continual re-solving (DeepStack [6]) and an abstracted blueprint refined by real-time subgame solving (Libratus [5], Pluribus [4]). ReBeL [1] turned the problem into one that the AlphaZero recipe can handle. It replaces world states with *public belief states* (PBS): the public observations together with a common-knowledge probability distribution over every player's private information. On a PBS a two-player zero-sum subgame has a unique value, so a value network can be trained by self-play and used at the leaves of depth-limited search, solved by CFR rather than Monte Carlo tree search. At test time the agent does not know the opponent's policy; it plays the policy of a randomly chosen CFR iteration, a Nash equilibrium in expectation, and with value error δ and T iterations plays a (δC₁ + δC₂/√T)-Nash equilibrium in any two-player zero-sum game [1]. ReBeL beat former competition champions and a top heads-up professional without card abstraction [1]. Its stated limits are a network input that grows linearly with the number of infostates per public state and guarantees "limited only to two-player zero-sum games" [1].

Student of Games (SoG) [2] extends this to perfect- and imperfect-information games with one algorithm. Growing-Tree CFR alternates CFR updates on the current public tree, with a value-and-policy network at the frontier, with PUCT-guided expansion of the tree; "sound self-play" trains the network on the outputs of mutually consistent searches. Its exploitability bound has the same shape as ReBeL's (a value-error term plus a CFR term decaying as 1/√T) [2]. SoG plays chess and Go at strong human level, beats the strongest openly available heads-up no-limit bot and the best Scotland Yard agent, but is weaker than AlphaZero at equal resources [2]. Its authors, too, restrict the analysis to two-player zero-sum games, where "the theoretical guarantee of Nash equilibria" is meaningful [2].

Across the five systems the representation moved from hand-built abstraction to learned values over beliefs, and computation moved from offline solving to search at decision time. Every system targets an approximate equilibrium. None of the landmark superhuman systems adapts to its opponents: Pluribus "plays a fixed strategy that does not adapt to the observed tendencies of the opponents" [4], and ReBeL's and SoG's test-time search is designed to be safe against an unknown opponent, not to exploit a known one [1, 2]. Where guarantees exist, they stop at two-player zero-sum games; Pluribus, the only system beyond two players, has none [4].

## Gaps
- **G-a (C1): the belief machinery of the landmark systems carries beliefs about cards, not about the opponent's strategy.** A PBS assumes that all policies are common knowledge [1, §3]. At test time ReBeL's fix is to play safely against an unknown policy, not to infer it [1, §6]; SoG likewise targets an approximate Nash equilibrium "with low exploitability" [2, Background]. Pluribus states that it does not adapt [4]. So none of the landmark superhuman systems adapts to its opponents. This must not be widened to "nobody adapts": opponent models have since been put inside depth-limited search [10], and small-game model-and-exploit agents exist (`lit_gaps.md`, C1). The open part for C1 is a *learned* opponent model inside a sound belief-state search, N-player and with within-match shifts.
- **G-b (C2): every equilibrium guarantee in the lineage stops at two-player zero-sum.** ReBeL's guarantees are "limited only to two-player zero-sum games" [1, §9]. SoG calls the Nash guarantee "less meaningful" beyond that setting [2]. Pluribus argues that N-player equilibria are not unique, are hard to compute, and do not combine when chosen independently, and it offers no guarantee [4]. This supports the landmark-systems half of the C2 gap only. N-player safety notions do exist elsewhere (equal share, team-maxmin; `lit_gaps.md`, C2), so Chapter I should say "the landmark systems' guarantees stop at two players", not "safety theory stops at two players".
- **G-c (C1 scaling): belief representations do not scale with private information.** ReBeL's input "grows linearly with the number of infostates in a public state" (intractable in Recon Chess) [1, §9]. SoG must enumerate "the information states per public state, which can be prohibitively expensive in some games" [2, Discussion]. Adding beliefs over opponent types, as C1 proposes, multiplies this cost, and no source addresses it.
- **G-d (C3): the evaluation protocols of these systems measure strength against fixed opponents.** They report head-to-head results against bots, a local-best-response probe, and (for ReBeL) one human over 7,500 hands with AIVAT [1, 2]. None measures adaptation, and none applies to more than two players. This is consistent with `lit_evaluation.md` (failure modes 1–3, 5).

## Lineage

DeepStack [6] and Libratus [5] reached superhuman heads-up no-limit play in 2017 by two routes. DeepStack solved depth-limited subgames with learned counterfactual values at the leaves (continual re-solving); Libratus refined an abstracted blueprint by nested safe subgame solving, which "comes with a provable safety guarantee" [5]. Brown, Sandholm and Amos then made depth-limited solving principled for imperfect information, where "states do not have well-defined values", by letting the opponent choose among several continuation strategies at the depth limit [3]. Pluribus applied that search on top of a blueprint to six-player poker, but its "algorithms … are not guaranteed to converge to a Nash equilibrium outside of two-player zero-sum games" [4]. ReBeL generalised DeepStack's "PBS value function" into self-play reinforcement learning plus search over public belief states, with a convergence proof for two-player zero-sum games [1, §2]. SoG, whose authors name ReBeL "the most closely related algorithm", combines DeepStack-style safe continual re-solving with AlphaZero-style guided tree growth and extends the same soundness to perfect-information games [2, Related Work]. At every step the target stayed an approximate equilibrium: in Brown and Sandholm's words, every superhuman game AI "was generated by attempting to approximate a Nash equilibrium strategy rather than by, for example, trying to detect and exploit weaknesses in the opponent" [4]. Search-plus-learning has since moved beyond two players, in CICERO's seven-player Diplomacy [9], but outside any equilibrium guarantee.
