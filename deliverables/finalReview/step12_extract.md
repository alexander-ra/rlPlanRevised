## Digest

Sequence models offer a route to game-playing agents that avoids value backups altogether. The Decision Transformer (DT) [1] casts reinforcement learning as supervised sequence modelling. A causally masked transformer is trained on offline trajectories of (return-to-go, state, action) triples; at test time it is conditioned on the return it should achieve. Paster et al. [2] showed that a return-conditioned policy reaches its target reliably only when conditioning on that target leaves the environment's transition probabilities unchanged (their Theorem 2.1). Otherwise, returns obtained by luck are imitated as if they had been earned. As a remedy they condition on the average returns of trajectory clusters. Opponents add a further source of variation that the agent does not control. The Adversarially Robust Decision Transformer (ARDT) [3] relabels each trajectory with a minimax return-to-go. This is a state–action value estimated by expectile regression, and with it ARDT recovers equilibrium strategies in sequential games with full data coverage. Its worst-case target is exact only under deterministic transitions, a limitation that a follow-up addresses in small synthetic stochastic games [4]. Poker combines both sources of noise, chance cards and hidden opponent holdings. A replication on Kuhn and Leduc poker found that return conditioning changes a DT's policy but does not steer it.

Transformers also serve directly as opponent models. In-context opponent modelling recognises fixed opponents from offline data [5], and, combined with decision-time search, it handles opponents that switch policy between episodes [6]. In heads-up poker, StratFormer [7] and AlphaExploitem [8] read action histories with transformers. They exploit sub-optimal opponents in Leduc (and Kuhn) while staying close to equilibrium against strong ones.

Large language models (LLMs) are a third route: the rules are given as text, and a pretrained model plays without game-specific training. A prompted GPT-4 agent that reasons explicitly about its opponent adapts its play in Leduc Hold'em [9]. Policy-level reflection improves play in Limit Texas Hold'em [10]. In Texas Hold'em agents, memory is needed before theory-of-mind-like exploitation emerges [11]. Open and proprietary LLMs nevertheless fail to approximate equilibrium play in Kuhn, Leduc and Limit Hold'em, and their actions diverge from their stated reasoning [12]. These studies score agents by chip gains or win rates against fixed baselines [9, 12, 13], so how much exploitability an LLM agent's exploitation costs goes unreported. On Kuhn, where both quantities are exact, a measurement shows the two moving in opposite directions.

## Gaps
Consistent with `lit_gaps.md` C1 ("narrowed substantially"): in-context and sequence-model opponent modelling exists and works in two-player games, so none of the points below claims that it is absent.
- **G-a (C1): sequence-model opponent modelling stops at two players and at switches between episodes.** Transformer opponent models recognise and exploit opponents in context [5, 6]. In poker they do so while staying near equilibrium [7, 8]. All of these are two-player. OMIS switches opponents only between episodes [6], and StratFormer lists non-stationary opponents as future work [7]. What remains open is lit_gaps C1 items 1–3: N-player adaptation paired with a safety criterion, shifts within a match, and a learned model coupled to a safe pipeline. Step 12 adds nothing N-player.
- **G-b (C1, if the framework is trained from logs): no return-conditioned or minimax-relabelled sequence model has been evaluated on imperfect-information card games.** The failure under environment stochasticity is characterised in [2]. ARDT [3] handles the adversary but assumes deterministic transitions; CART [4] handles chance only in synthetic stage games (workshop paper). Neither is tested on poker, where the deal and hidden cards act as chance. No counter-example was found, but the search was narrow: *DTCard* (a DT framework for trick-taking card games, MDPI *Appl. Sci.* 16(7), 3117, 2026) could not be read (HTTP 403) and must be checked before this is claimed.
- **G-c (C1 → C3): LLM poker agents are evaluated by chips or win rate, not by exploitability or speed of adaptation.** This holds for [9, 12, 13] and matches `lit_evaluation.md` failure mode 9. Evidence on adaptation is mixed. Prompted frontier models with explicit opponent reasoning or memory adapt [9, 11]. A small open model given raw history does not, yet does respond when told the opponent's type (Own evidence). No protocol reports exploitation gain and worst-case exposure together for such agents, which is the gap C3 targets.
- **G-d (C3, methodological requirement rather than a literature gap): an LLM's self-report cannot serve as the measurement.** [12] documents actions that diverge from stated reasoning, and step 12 finds stated frequencies *more* exploitable than played ones. Strategies must be extracted behaviourally (log-probabilities or sampling).
- **G-e (C3): deviation size is not cost.** A per-information-set decomposition of exploitability showed the largest deviation costing 0.1 % and one node costing 41.4 %. I did not search the literature for per-decision exploitability diagnostics, so this must not be presented as a gap until that search is done.

## Own evidence
All numbers come from the candidate's own runs of 25–27 July 2026 (committed JSON under `implementation/step12/implementation/results/`). The models were run locally with LM Studio 0.4.20 on an RTX 5090 at temperature 0.7: gpt-oss-20b (MoE, 21B total / 3.6B active), Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct, and OpenThinker3-7B (an SFT of Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct). The GGUF quantisation of the Qwen and OpenThinker weights was not recorded. No frontier or API model was tested.
- **Return conditioning does not steer.** On Kuhn, DT exploitability conditioned on +2 vs −2 is 0.6981 vs 0.6928 chips, with a spike to ≈ 1.85–1.98 at the modal return −1. On Leduc, Pearson r = +0.062 (Spearman −0.054) across 16 targets vs a near-Nash opponent. Sources: `logs/validate_smoke.log`, `results/dt_experiments_SMOKE.json`, `results/leduc_stage0.json`. Caveat: SMOKE profile (5,000 trajectories, small DT), one training run per point; DT exploitability varies 0.67–0.80 between retrains.
- **Cloning beats return conditioning.** Behavioural cloning reaches 0.055 chips vs 0.799 for the DT and 0.469 for the ARDT proxy (Nash 0.016). Source: `results/comparison_SMOKE_stub.json`. Caveat: the ARDT here is a state-only V(s) simplification, not the published Q̃(s,a) method.
- **Exploitation and exploitability diverge.** Against a six-bot Kuhn zoo, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct wins 0.177 chips/hand vs 0.110 for CFR equilibrium, at an exploitability of 0.357 vs 0.006. About 90 % of the advantage comes from the passive and random bots. Source: `results/exploitation_qwen2.5-7b-instruct.json`. Caveat: a fixed strategy extracted with 12 model calls, one seed, 4,000 hands per bot.
- **No inference from history, but response to description.** With the last 20 hands in context (CoT, 120 hands), Qwen2.5-7B captures 83 % of the available exploitation against AlwaysPass in both halves (0.824 → 0.827), and the other two cells are underpowered (SE ±0.53–0.80). When told the opponent's type, the models shift their Jack bluff rate by +0.21 to +0.92. Sources: `results/opponent_modeling_qwen2.5-7b-instruct.json`, `comparison_SMOKE_*.json` (`adaptation_delta`). Caveat: one model for the history test, three opponents.
- **Stated vs played strategy.** Frequencies the models state, if played, are 0.921 vs 0.357 chips exploitable (Qwen2.5-7B) and 1.576 vs 0.392 (gpt-oss-20b). Source: `results/frequency_elicitation_*.json`. Caveat: Kuhn only, 3 elicitation repeats, two models.
- **Deviation ≠ cost.** For Qwen2.5-7B, one Queen node (P2, after a check) carries 41.4 % of the 0.357 chips of exploitability, while King root betting at 1.00 carries 0.1 %. Source: `results/decomposition_qwen2.5-7b-instruct_plain_logprob.json`. Caveat: deviation is measured against one member of Kuhn's equilibrium family (King bet 0.56 at 50k CFR iterations; 0.68 at 5k).
- **Toy-game optimism.** On Leduc, Qwen2.5-7B scores −0.463 ± 0.132 vs near-Nash, indistinguishable from the DT's −0.454. Against weak bots it averages −0.071 (Nash +0.582). 23.4 % of its probability mass targets illegal actions, 99.97 % of that folding when a check is free. Sources: `results/leduc_llm_*.json`, `results/leduc_illegal_taxonomy_*.json`. Caveat: one model, one prompt style, 600 hands.
